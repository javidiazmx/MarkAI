# How Much Do Property Managers Charge in Chicago? The Break-Even Math on a 7–10% Fee

**Short answer:** property management fees in Chicago and the surrounding suburbs run **7–10% of collected rent** for full-service residential management, plus a tenant placement fee — usually about half the first month's rent, though 75% to 100% is not uncommon — and a fee at each renewal. On a $2,200 rental that is roughly **$1,848 to $2,640 a year**.

Now the part most fee articles skip.

A 7–10% fee costs about one month's rent a year. One vacant month costs about 8–10% of annual rent. Those two numbers sit almost on top of each other. So the real question was never "what do managers charge." It is whether self-managing adds more vacant days and more ordinance exposure than the fee saves.

Plenty of Chicagoland owners self-manage well, and there is a section below on when you should not hire anyone, us included. Every figure on this page comes from our own published material, and we say where each one came from.

## The break-even number: price the fee in vacant days, not percent

Percent is the wrong unit. No landlord experiences 8%. They experience a month with no deposit in the account.

### A 7–10% fee is 25 to 36 days of rent a year

Run it. An 8% fee across twelve months is 0.96 of one month's rent a year. At 7% it is 0.84 of a month — about 25 days of rent. At 10% it is 1.2 months, about 36 days.

So a full year of management costs roughly one month of vacancy. That is the whole conversion.

### One vacant month costs about what a full year of management costs

Each month without a tenant costs roughly **8–10% of annual rent**, per our Schaumburg guide. The fee is 7–10% of collected rent. The ranges overlap almost entirely.

That is arithmetic laid across two published ranges, not a controlled study. But the shape holds: prevent one vacant month a year and the fee has paid for itself before anything else is counted.

### Self-managed leasing runs 10 to 21 days longer

In our own analysis, self-managed leasing tends to run 10 to 21 days longer than professionally managed leasing — slower listing syndication, less competitive pricing analysis, less efficient showing coordination. Nothing dramatic. Just friction, measured in days.

Price it on your unit. Daily vacancy cost is rent divided by 30.

On a $1,500 rental that is $50 a day:

- 10 extra days = **$500**
- 15 extra days = **$750**
- 21 extra days = **$1,050**

Against an annual fee of $1,260 at 7% or $1,800 at 10%, that is between about a quarter and most of a year's fee — spent, in a turnover year, before you count a single hour of your own time. At the 15-day midpoint it is 40% to 60% of the fee on lease-up speed alone.

Use your own rent: divide by 30, multiply by the days. The $50 belongs to $1,500, not to you.

For a benchmark on what "normal" looks like: across the Chicago metro in early 2025, single-family homes leased in 17 days and multifamily units in 20. Suburban numbers run longer — 18 to 25 days for condos and townhomes and 25 to 45 for houses in Skokie; 22 to 30 and 30 to 45 in Oak Lawn.

Two more timing numbers worth stealing: marketing should start **30 to 60 days** before the unit goes vacant, which is the window apartment-search experts recommend. And across our 2025 leasing numbers, vacancy cost roughly **$733 in lost revenue per turnover** on a $2,000 rental.

## Should you self-manage? Run these six numbers

This is the fork in the road. Here it is as a table.

| Line item | Self-managing | Professionally managed |
|---|---|---|
| Annual fee on $1,500 rent | $0 — you keep the ~10% | $1,260–$1,800 |
| Days to lease | 10–21 days longer | — |
| Cost of those extra days, **per turnover** | $500–$1,050 | — |
| Your hours, stabilized year, no maintenance | 15–25 a year | ~0 |
| Your hours in a turnover year | 40–60 **per turn**, on top of the above | ~0 |
| Rent pricing | Your judgment | A recommended rent range of about $200 on every unit we manage |

Two notes on that table, because the honest version is more useful than the flattering one.

The vacancy cost is **per turnover, not annual**. On a long-held stable tenancy, the fee is genuinely harder to justify on vacancy math alone — which is exactly the argument the "when you should not hire us" section makes below. And the 15–25 hours excludes maintenance entirely; the 30–40 hours a year we quote covers the whole process including leasing, which is why the two figures do not match. We would rather name that than hide it.

Three costs owners leave out of the comparison every time:

**Hours.** 15 to 25 a year on a stabilized property with no maintenance. 40 to 60 per turn, plus possibly a full month of commission if you use a realtor to re-rent.

**Turnover.** A tenant who renews for one more year instead of moving saves roughly one month of vacancy *plus* $2,000 to $4,000 in turnover and leasing costs. Retention is the single biggest lever on the statement, and it is almost entirely a function of how fast maintenance gets handled.

**Pricing.** From our own portfolio: units that refuse pets take about two weeks longer to lease and get **1.8% less rent**, because the price has to drop to stay attractive. Separately from any pet policy — assistance and emotional support animals are not pets under the Fair Housing Act, a no-pets policy does not override that, and Illinois has had its own Assistance Animal Integrity Act since 2019.

## Property management fees outside the city

Most fee articles say "Chicago" and mean the city. Most Chicagoland rentals are not in it. Here is the part no competing page covers, because the cost outside the city is not really the percentage — it is the licensing.

| Area | Typical fee | Governing ordinance | Licensing to budget for |
|---|---|---|---|
| City of Chicago | ~10% is the figure self-managers say they avoid | Chicago CRLTO | City requirements vary by unit count |
| Suburban Cook | 5–8% | Cook County RTLO | Village by village — Hoffman Estates and Schaumburg $150/yr, Elk Grove Village $50–$100 |
| Oak Park, Evanston, Mount Prospect | 5–8% | Each has its own ordinance; the county RTLO does **not** apply | Village by village |
| DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane, McHenry, Kendall | 5–8% | No RTLO; Illinois statute plus village rules | Elgin $149 up to $448 for a 25-unit building, Bensenville $211, Aurora $90 |

The license fee is rarely the problem. **The clock is.** On a rental going through licensing for the first time, the inspection process can run **14 to 45 days** — and on a unit sitting empty, those are vacant days. A 45-day hold on a $1,500 unit is about $2,250 if it stands between you and a signed lease. Add reinspection fees of $75 to $150, license application delays and surprise citations — a tenant's RV in the driveway, grass too high, a tilted mailbox — and a 10% ROI becomes a 3% return fast.

## What ordinance mistakes cost, by jurisdiction

No fee comparison on the internet includes this column, and it is usually the biggest one.

Find your building first. Chicago, suburban Cook and Evanston are not interchangeable, and applying the wrong one is a wrong answer, not a near miss.

- **City of Chicago** — the Chicago CRLTO.
- **Suburban Cook County** — the Cook County RTLO, effective June 1, 2021, covering the 125 Cook municipalities that did not already have their own.
- **Oak Park, Evanston, Mount Prospect** — each has its own comprehensive ordinance. The county RTLO does not reach inside them.
- **DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane, McHenry, Kendall** — no RTLO. Illinois statute plus whatever the village requires.

Two Cook County rules reach almost every building in the county, including ones otherwise exempt: the **anti-lockout prohibition** applies to all dwelling units, and the **Just Housing Amendment** applies to every Cook County owner, manager and broker, with no exemption for building size, location, or using a third-party screening service. Neither travels into DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane, McHenry or Kendall.

### Three different late-fee caps inside one metro

- **Chicago (CRLTO 5-12-140(h))** — $10 on the first $500 of monthly rent, plus 5% of anything above $500.
- **Suburban Cook (RTLO)** — $10 on the first $1,000, plus 5% above $1,000.
- **Evanston (RLTO 5-3-3(A)(8))** — $25 on the first $1,600, plus 5% above. On $2,000 rent, no more than $45.

For a voucher holder, the fee applies only to the resident's portion of rent — so where that portion is $500 or less in Chicago, it never exceeds $10.

Get it wrong and the ordinance hands the tenant the money. In Chicago, enforcing an illegal late fee lets a tenant recover **two months' rent plus attorney's fees** — more than a year of management on most units, over a fee you charged to make a point. [The breakdown by municipality is here](https://www.gcrealtyinc.com/blog/late-fees-in-chicago-cook-county-evanston--everywhere-else).

### Deposits, where the clerical part is the expensive part

Illinois does not allow a non-refundable security deposit. Full stop.

In suburban Cook, the RTLO caps deposits at **1.5 times monthly rent**, the tenant can pay that extra half-month in installments over the first six months, and once a tenant gives written notice of a breach the landlord has **two business days** to remedy it. Miss that window and the tenant is awarded **two times the deposit plus attorney fees**.

Chicago's CRLTO is the stricter operational job: a separate interest-bearing account at an Illinois institution, not commingled; written disclosure of the bank and account; interest paid annually at the City-set rate; itemized deductions with paid receipts attached within 30 days of move-out; the balance refunded within 45. Underpay the interest by a few cents and the exposure is still two times the deposit plus legal fees.

Which is why many Chicago owners charge a move-in fee instead, typically 25–35% of rent. One tripwire there: **a non-refundable move-in fee above 49% of one month's rent is a security deposit**, no matter what the lease or the ad says — and then every rule above applies to it retroactively.

### Entry and renewal timing — the rules hands-on owners break

In suburban Cook, a tenant can recover **up to two months' rent or twice the damages sustained, whichever is greater, plus attorney's fees, for each unauthorized entry**. The owners most exposed to this are the ones who self-manage because they live nearby and like to stop by.

Notice timing is the other one, and being early is also a violation.

**Asking a tenant to commit** to a renewal: no more than 90 days out in Chicago, 60 in Cook County. Push past it in Chicago and it can cost a month's rent; in Cook County, a month's rent or actual damages, whichever is greater.

**The notice you owe them** runs on a different and earlier clock:

- **Chicago** — the Fair Notice Ordinance requires 30, 60 or 120 days depending on how long the tenant has lived there: 60 days past six months, 120 days past three years. (It does not apply to nonpayment, material lease violations, disturbance or abandonment.)
- **Suburban Cook** — 60 days before the stated termination date to end a month-to-month tenancy or decline renewal. Miss it and you must give the tenant 120 days from the date of notice, **at the same rent**, for the whole period.
- **Evanston** — 90 days.
- **Elsewhere in Illinois** — 30 days is the statutory default, and it applies only where no local ordinance sets a longer period.

That is a calendar problem before it is a legal one, which is why in parts of Chicago we start the renewal process 150 days out — preparing the notice and the pricing, not asking the tenant to commit, which cannot happen that early.

### Screening, fair housing and application fees

Fair housing exposure is the one number in this article nobody should be casual about. We have put the range at **$10,000 to $50,000 or more per offense**; it varies by whether the case is federal, state or local and whether it is a first offense.

The defense is a written standard, applied identically to every applicant, written down before the first application arrives. Ours is published: a **600 minimum credit score and net income of at least 2.5× rent** as the baseline, with a stricter standard on some Chicago units — 700 or greater, net income of 4× rent, three recent pay stubs, six months at current employment, no past-due balances, and positive rental or mortgage history within the last 18 months.

In Cook County, uniform application is necessary but not sufficient. Two rules sit on top of it:

**Source of income is protected.** Chicago and Cook County since October 2013, and statewide in Illinois since January 1, 2023. You cannot refuse an applicant because the rent arrives as a housing voucher — and an income multiple is applied to the resident's portion of rent, not to the full contract rent.

**The Just Housing Amendment requires a two-step screening.** Non-criminal factors first. Criminal history only in a second review, after the applicant has cleared the rest — and once you notify an applicant that their background report contains criminal history that could lead to denial, you must observe a **five-day pause**. Before you accept any application fee at all, you must give the applicant your written tenant selection criteria, notice of their right to dispute criminal-history information and to offer evidence of rehabilitation, and contact details for the Cook County Commission on Human Rights.

On the fee itself: the Chicago market charges **$30 to $75 per applicant over 18**. Our own published material reports the **$50 Illinois cap, inclusive of the background-check cost**, both as current law and as an HB 3564 provision taking effect January 2027 — we are telling you that plainly rather than papering over it. Pricing at or below $50, with receipts, is the safe position either way. Confirm the effective date for your situation before charging more.

### What changes in January 2027 (HB 3564)

[HB 3564 is now law](https://www.gcrealtyinc.com/blog/hb-3564-is-now-law-january-2027-changes-for-all-illinois-landlords), effective January 2027.

- A fee **not listed on the first page of the lease** is not collectable.
- Eleven fee types are banned outright, including maintenance hotline fees, travel to complete repairs, and move-in or move-out walkthroughs.
- Any fee or fine for modifying or renewing a lease is banned regardless of disclosure.
- An application fee above $50 will be permitted only where the actual third-party cost is higher, the landlord pays it upfront, and receipts are provided within 14 days — and missing that 14-day window waives the fee entirely.

Two limits worth knowing. It does not apply to owner-occupied buildings of six units or fewer. And it governs what a landlord charges a **tenant**, not what a manager charges an **owner**.

## What one bad tenancy actually costs in Cook County

Filing an eviction in Cook County runs **$287 to $500** depending on how many tenants live there — against a US average around $50. As of our 2024 review, attorney fees ran **$800 to $2,100** depending on court dates and complexity, unchanged from our 2023 review. Check current court fees with the Clerk of the Circuit Court before you budget; these move.

Waiting out the system with a non-paying tenant can exceed **$5,000** in hard cost, and that figure does not price the part landlords actually describe to us, which is what it does to your year.

**Here is the detail that changes the math.** In a nonpayment case, a Chicago tenant can dismiss the eviction and stay by paying the unpaid rent plus the landlord's court **filing** fees, at any time before a judge issues an order of possession. Attorney's fees are not on that list. You can spend $2,100 getting to a courtroom and recover none of it.

Which is why cash for keys is often the cheaper decision rather than the soft one: if a contested eviction might cost $3,000 in attorney fees plus months of unpaid rent, offering that same $3,000 to the tenant can end it in under a month.

Serving the wrong notice is a 45-day delay at minimum before you are back at the start. A process server costs $75 to $100. And after a bad tenancy, cosmetic repairs run $2,000 to $5,000, damage to appliances, plumbing and HVAC runs $5,000 to $15,000 or more, and a full rehab after severe neglect starts around $20,000.

## When you should not hire a property manager

We publish a whole post on [when property management is not worth the cost](https://www.gcrealtyinc.com/blog/when-is-property-management-not-worth-the-cost), and this is the honest summary of it.

Self-managing is the better call when most of these are true:

- You live close enough to handle a showing or a lockout the same day.
- You have one stable long-term tenant, and this is not a turnover year.
- You are genuinely handy, or you already have a plumber who answers.
- You have the 15 to 25 hours a year — and the 40 to 60 if the unit turns.
- You already run a renewal process that starts 90 or more days before expiration.
- You can recite the deposit, late-fee and notice rules for your specific municipality without looking them up.

Hit those and you keep the roughly 10% a manager would charge — about **$1,800 a year on a $1,500 unit**. That is a real number and it is yours. Take it.

Come back when one of those changes: a turnover year, a move out of the area, a second property, or the first time a notice actually has to be served.

## What to ask before you sign with anyone, including us

**Start with the license.** In Illinois, anyone providing property management services for others must hold a valid real estate broker's license or work under someone who does. Self-managing owners are generally exempt. Ask for the managing broker's license number and verify it with the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation. That clears unlicensed operators off your shortlist before price ever comes up.

Then, seven questions:

1. Is the fee charged on rent **collected** or rent **due**?
2. What is the leasing fee, as a percentage of the first month's rent?
3. Is the renewal fee flat or a percentage? (Ours is a flat $125.)
4. Do you mark up maintenance invoices, and by how much?
5. What can you spend without calling me? (Ours: work over $1,000, insurance claims and major systems come to you first.)
6. What is the maximum renewal lead time in my municipality, and what is your average days on market for a property like mine? (A good answer names 90 days in Chicago, 60 in Cook County, without having to check.)
7. What is the agreement term, and what does it take to cancel?

**Normalize every proposal to one annual dollar figure before you compare them.** A 7% fee with a 100% leasing fee and a 20% maintenance markup can easily cost more than a 9% fee with neither. Walk away from anyone who will not put the markup in writing or cannot produce a license number.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much do property managers charge in Chicago?

7–10% of collected rent for full-service residential management, plus a tenant placement fee of 50–100% of the first month's rent and a fee at each renewal. On a $2,200 rental, roughly $1,848 to $2,640 a year.

### Is the fee based on rent collected or rent due?

The Chicagoland benchmark is quoted against rent **collected**, which means the manager earns when you do. Some agreements are written against rent due instead. Ask, and get the answer in the contract.

### What does the monthly management fee actually cover?

At the 7–8% end of the range: leasing, inspections, rent collection, and compliance including the Cook County RTLO and the crime-free seminar where a village requires one. We put that at 30 to 40 hours a year across the full process.

### Do property managers mark up maintenance invoices?

Some do, in the range of 15% to 25%, which we have documented in both our 2025 and 2026 reviews of Chicago companies. It is the most common way a low headline rate becomes an expensive year. Ask question 4 above.

### Do you need a license to be a property manager in Illinois?

Yes, to manage for others — a real estate broker's license, or working under someone who holds one. Owners managing their own property are generally exempt. Verify the number with IDFPR.

### How much does an eviction cost in Cook County?

$287 to $500 to file, plus $800 to $2,100 in attorney fees as of our 2024 review. A worst-case wait exceeds $5,000 — and attorney fees are not recoverable when a tenant cures by paying rent plus filing fees.

### When is a property manager not worth the cost?

When you live nearby, have one stable long-term tenant, are handy, have the 15 to 25 hours a year, and know your municipality's deposit, late-fee and notice rules cold. Then you keep about $1,800 a year on a $1,500 unit, and you have earned it.
